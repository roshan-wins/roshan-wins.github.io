The question

When is a two-state bicycle model good enough, and when do you need a full multibody model? Our group built both for a Volvo S90 and drove them with the same steering inputs the real car saw on the Lunda airfield track.

Three models and one car

  • Bicycle model, MATLAB. Lateral velocity and yaw rate as states, with either linear tires or Fiala brush tires that saturate at μFz. The measured steering angle and speed drive it.
  • Full vehicle, Adams Car. Double-wishbone front and multi-link rear, both with anti-roll bars; PAC2002 tires for 255/40 R19; a steering ratio of 16.3; brakes and powertrain. The assembled model weighs 2007 kg, against 1995 kg in the bicycle model.
  • Track data. A VBOX GPS/IMU logged steering angle, velocities, yaw rate and accelerations at 100 Hz through ramp steer, step steer, double lane change and slalom runs between 50 and 90 km/h.

Calibrating the tires

Axle cornering stiffness sets how hard the car responds, so we tried three front and rear pairs against the 90 km/h slalom. With 100 / 400 kN/rad both bicycle models fell well short of the car, at about 10 deg/s of yaw rate and under 5 m/s² of lateral acceleration. With 300 / 200 kN/rad the linear model overshot to about 40 deg/s and the Fiala model lost its rear axle and spun at 1.6 s. 200 / 300 kN/rad matched the track data, so those values stayed for the rest of the study. The simulator above lets you try all three.

What the data showed

  • Below about 3.5 m/s² of lateral acceleration the linear bicycle model matches the car.
  • In the 90 km/h slalom the real car saturates at about 8.5 m/s². The linear model keeps climbing past 10 m/s²; the Fiala and Adams models follow the plateau.
  • Rigid-link hand calculations underestimate wheel rates because they leave out bushing stiffness: 18.4 against 25 kN/m at the front and 29.1 against 70.3 kN/m at the rear in parallel wheel travel.
  • In the sharp reversal of the 90 km/h double lane change, Adams Car tracks the measurement most closely.

Mass and centre of gravity

With the Adams model validated, we changed the mass (−100, +100 and +200 kg) and moved the centre of gravity (−50, +50 and +100 mm) in step steers at 50 and 80 km/h. A lighter car and a rearward centre of gravity both raise the yaw response, and both effects grow with speed.

After the report

I kept working on the Adams Car model once the report was in. With modified suspension parameters, the model ran the standard slalom test 5 seconds faster than the real car did in our test at the Lunda track. Those runs came after the report, so the figures above do not include them.

ABOUT THE SIMULATOR

It uses the bicycle model's equations and parameters from the report (1995 kg, yaw inertia 3090 kg·m², wheelbase 2.941 m, steering ratio 16.3) and integrates them in your browser. Unlike the report, it holds speed constant and uses a synthetic steering input instead of the recorded one.
