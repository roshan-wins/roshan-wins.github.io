Aero devices for high-performance cars

How rear wings, side skirts, canards and rear diffusers make downforce, and how they work together: theory, CFD of side skirts on a simplified body, and the Porsche 911 GT3 RS and McLaren 720S as case studies.

Why downforce

Aerodynamic forces grow with the square of speed while mechanical grip stays roughly constant, so above a certain speed aerodynamics decides how much load the tires can carry. Rear wings, side skirts, canards and rear diffusers are four ways of turning the oncoming air into downforce without adding mass. The study covered the physics of each, how they interact, CFD of side skirts on a simplified body, and two production cars, the Porsche 911 GT3 RS and the McLaren 720S.

Four devices, one floor

  • Rear wing. An inverted airfoil that makes downforce directly, at the cost of drag because it sits in the free stream. Downforce rises with angle of attack until the wing stalls.
  • Side skirts. A physical seal along the sills that throttles high-pressure air leaking into the low-pressure underbody. The gap to the road matters: too large and the seal leaks, too small and the skirt itself can separate the flow.
  • Canards. Small surfaces at the front corners whose main job is the tip vortex they shed, which pulls the turbulent wheel wake away from the floor edge.
  • Rear diffuser. A gently expanding duct that slows the underbody flow back to free-stream pressure. Too steep an angle and the flow separates, and the downforce goes with it.

None of them works alone: the canard vortex runs along the skirt line, the skirts keep the floor flow clean, and the wing's low-pressure field helps pull air out of the diffuser.

CFD of the side skirts

A baseline body was modelled in SolidWorks and meshed with polyhedral cells, with refinement around the wheels and the lower body edge and prism layers sized for y⁺ ≈ 1 so the turbulence model resolves the viscous sublayer. Pathlines show the skirts blocking the cross-flow from the sides and keeping a faster, lower-pressure core under the floor.

My part: the rear diffuser

I did the diffuser part of the study. In short:

  • How it works. The diffuser is the expansion that answers the contraction at the splitter and the sealed floor: a controlled divergent duct that lets the fast, low-pressure underbody flow slow down and recover to free-stream pressure. That recovery is an adverse pressure gradient, so the expansion has to be gentle enough to keep the boundary layer attached.
  • Where it fails. Too steep an angle and the flow separates from the diffuser walls: the diffuser stalls and the downforce drops suddenly. CFD studies on production-like shapes point to the angle and the ride height as the variables that matter most, with moderate angles of about 7–10° the usual compromise, and curved profiles delaying separation further than straight ones.
  • On a real car. The McLaren 720S treats the floor and diffuser as one system and uses the low-pressure wake behind the car to pull flow out of the diffuser exit, which gives ground-effect downforce without the drag of a large wing.
  • What it depends on. Ride height and rake. If the rear lifts under hard braking, the effective expansion angle grows, the diffuser can stall and the rear loses downforce at once. That is why future designs are likely to pair diffusers with active suspension and better underbody sealing.
