The problem

An assembly-line nutrunner has to control both speed and torque: fast while the nut runs down, slower once the joint starts to clamp, and off at exactly the target torque. We made the model for a KTH group assignment. It starts from a real cordless angle nutrunner (Atlas Copco Tensor ITB-A61, 10–55 N·m, 1500 W) and a Faulhaber motor assigned to each group, and builds the model up one piece at a time.

How the assignment builds it up

  • Motor first. The voltage-driven DC motor in five forms (differential equations, a Simulink block diagram, state space, transfer function and a Simscape model), checked against each other and against the datasheet's stall torque, no-load speed and mechanical time constant.
  • Speed control. A PI angular-velocity controller for the motor alone, tuned for a fast step response without overshoot and within the current limit.
  • Simplify. Pole locations of the motor model justify dropping the electrical dynamics, and the reduced model is rebuilt in all forms.
  • Gearing. Two equal planetary stages, sized from the motor's stall torque because the tool never runs continuously.
  • Rundown. A fourth-order model of the drivetrain and free-running nut, and a PI controller that follows the speed profile without overshoot or steady-state error.
  • Clamping. An M8, class 8.8 bolt (1.25 mm pitch, 100 mm grip) turns into a torsional stiffness through the thread pitch. A velocity controller with a torque cut-off stops at the target, first 25 N·m, then rechecked against the bolt's yield strength once thread friction is added.
  • Energy. Electrical energy per tightening, and how many bolts one 36 V, 2.5 Ah battery charge covers.

About the tuner

The tuner above runs the brief's model in your browser. It is a fresh implementation of that model, written for this page. I used motor 4 from the brief's list, a Faulhaber 4490 036 B at 36 V. The two planetary stages give 20 : 1 in total, which lets its stall torque reach the tool's 55 N·m. The transmission stiffness and damping and the nut friction are the brief's values, and the inertia of the output shafts, socket and nut is estimated. The controller samples at 1 kHz with one sample of delay, so too much gain makes the speed ring the way a real digital controller would.

Try proportional gain on its own first. The nut runs slow, because friction needs a steady voltage and a P controller only makes voltage from error. Then switch to tightening. With the brief's 25 N·m target and thread friction, the bolt is pulled past its yield load, the case the last task of the assignment asks you to fix by lowering the target.
